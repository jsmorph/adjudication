‘Inmates Taking The Asylum’—Polymarket’s $16M Clavicular Bet

A long-time Polymarket bettor named Domahhhh published a four-screenshot thread on X on April 30 calling the prediction market’s oracle “a disinformation engine” run by “rogue traders,” and the on-chain data behind his complaint is striking. The disputed market, “Clavicular pregnancy in 2026?”, has done $16.46 million in lifetime volume on Polymarket, including roughly $4.5 million in the past 24 hours alone, according to the platform’s command-line tool. Yes shares trade at 97 cents. The market is currently flagged disputed on UMA, the decentralized oracle Polymarket uses to settle outcomes, and it has cycled through two full rounds of proposed → disputed since being created on April 20.
The argument is over what counts as a credible pregnancy announcement from a streamer who told his audience he had gotten a girl pregnant. The market’s own rules state that “pregnancy announcements that are not credible, for example jokes, will not suffice.” Other traders posting through the dispute, including @holy_moses7, said the market had been “confirmed to resolve as ‘No’ two days ago.” Yes shares went up anyway.
“Hopefully Polymarket is on precipice of replacing UMA, because the ‘oracle’ that underpins the site is now a disinformation engine that has been taken over by rogue traders,” Domahhhh wrote in the April 30 thread, which had pulled 15,100 views, 267 likes and 54 replies within the first few hours. He has bet on political markets full-time since 2007 and rarely posts on X. His feed is mostly silent between large Polymarket disputes.
The Evidence
The Clavicular market is where the money sits. According to Polymarket’s holder data pulled at 18:50 UTC on April 30, the top 20 wallets on the Yes side hold a combined $2.54 million in shares. The single largest, an account named HorribleWork at wallet 0x78048Aa26a…, holds $594,694 in Yes, equivalent to about 3.6% of all volume the market has ever recorded. The next four Yes whales (alliswell, JAHODA, zidanmuhammadalhusseini and tourists) each hold between $200,000 and $345,000. The top 20 No holders, by contrast, hold $1.49 million combined; the largest, ScottyNooo, holds $207,643. Yes whales have roughly $1 million more on the line than No whales.
The dispute price action has been violent. The Polymarket CLI shows Yes shares moved up 73 cents over the past week, from around 24 cents to 97 cents. On April 28, they dropped from 97% to 50% before reversing. A single 299,000-share No buy at 99.7 cents on April 29 ($299,000 at the No price implies a near-total bet that the dispute will eventually resolve No) was flagged on X by the prediction-markets account @JexTrade.
MORE FOR YOU
“A Polymarket whale bought $299,000 shares of Clavicular pregnancy at 99.7 cents,” @JexTrade posted late on April 29. “Is UMA gonna rug the whales on this next vote?”
Other large traders piled into the thread. “Complete shitshow,” wrote @GollumGekko1, identified by Domahhhh as a top-0.1% Polymarket account. A reply from @hellomudda6942 said the issue was not unique: “The Israel x Hezbollah ceasefire markets were scammed too. I lost $12,000 because UMA says Lebanon is the same as Hezbollah.” A separate user, @holy_moses7, took the opposing view, arguing the Yes buying was suspect: “Some dude is on a schizo campaign, donating over $70,000 to Polymarket traders. He’s buying into a market that was confirmed to resolve as ‘No’ two days ago.”
Across the orderbook, the disagreement is binary. The market description and the platform clarification say No. The price says Yes. The oracle, after two dispute rounds, has not closed the gap.
The Pattern
This is not the first time UMA has come under fire from Polymarket’s most active traders, and it is not the largest sum in dispute.
In late March 2026, a Polymarket market on whether President Trump had spoken to President Xi Jinping during the month swung from roughly 17% Yes to 95% Yes after a small set of UMA voters cast their tokens for Yes, despite no public confirmation from China. “Sadly, a few influential Polymarket/UMA voters can basically decide the outcome of markets,” [wrote @TheGreekTrader](https://x.com/TheGreekTrader/status/2038561737117438153), a Polymarket trader who has documented turning a $150 deposit into $70,000. “At this point, markets aren’t resolving based on clear evidence, but based on what a few people like ‘JessicaOnlyChild’ or ‘UMA rocks’ think.” Polymarket overrode the UMA vote and clarified the market resolved No. Domahhhh, who was active in that dispute too, wrote at the time: “The fact that uma rocks is influential and using that influence to overturn reality is totally bonkers. UMA is deader than dead.”
A top-0.01% Polymarket trader who posts as @EpsilonPM was blunter about UMA’s design. “It’s not a ‘decentralized’ oracle; it’s purely a proxy for the will of Jessica & UMA Rocks,” he wrote on March 30. “I have far more faith in the integrity of the Polymarket team.”
The most expensive precedent is older. In March 2025, a $7 million Polymarket market on whether the United States and Ukraine had signed a critical-minerals deal flipped to 100% Yes and resolved Yes despite no signed agreement. Polymarket called it “unprecedented” and a “governance attack” in a public statement, according to The Block, but declined to issue refunds. Reporting from The Defiant, CoinDesk and Decrypt traced the manipulation to a single UMA whale operating across three wallets and holding roughly 5 million UMA tokens, or about a quarter of active voting power at the time. “Polymarket’s silence on the issue has been deafening and perhaps even inculpatory,” Norway-based equity analyst Folke Hermansen, who first flagged the manipulation, said in a March 2025 post on X. Domahhhh’s original investigation thread on that case is the same account whose Clavicular complaint is now circulating, and he has framed the new dispute as a sequel: “It’s now been a year since the minerals market heist, where users lost millions of dollars to a fraudulent UMA scheme. UMA is far more vulnerable than it was a year ago, and the inmates are starting to take the asylum.”
A separate UMA dispute is still hanging over a $254 million market on whether the US-Iran ceasefire was extended past April 22, according to Polymarket’s own data and coverage in Blockonomi. Trump declared an indefinite extension on April 21; Iran did not issue a direct public communique, leaving Yes shares trading at 0.15 cents on Polymarket despite widespread wire-service reporting on the extension. The vote there has not been settled.
How UMA Is Supposed To Work
UMA’s “Optimistic Oracle,” built by 2018 founders Hart Lambur and Allison Lu, both former Goldman Sachs traders, runs in three stages. A bonded proposer posts a Yes/No assertion. A liveness window, usually two hours to two days for Polymarket, lets anyone dispute it with a counter-bond. If a dispute happens, UMA’s Data Verification Mechanism opens a token-holder vote that runs 48 to 96 hours.
The design’s known weakness is the incentive: the DVM rewards Schelling-point consensus rather than independent accuracy. Stakers who vote with the eventual minority are slashed about 0.1% of their stake per vote, with the slashed tokens redistributed to the majority. According to UMA’s own documentation, voting needs 65% of staked tokens to clear the system’s “SPAT” threshold and at least 5 million UMA participating to clear the “GAT.” The result is a structure that punishes dissent even from voters who turn out to be factually right.
UMA tightened the proposer side after the minerals attack. In August 2025, governance proposal UMIP-189 upgraded Polymarket to a “Managed Optimistic Oracle V2,” whitelisting an initial 37 addresses as the only authorized proposers (later expanded to 177 by November 2025). According to UMA’s blog, incorrect proposals fell 59% and disputes fell 68% in the first month. The patch did not change DVM voting, which remains permissionless. Disputes still escalate to a token-holder vote any UMA holder can join. A UMA-affiliated account, @BenniDaytime, posted during the Trump-Xi dispute that voters should disregard outside framing of the question, writing: “this is an issue of market rules.”
What Polymarket Is Building Next
Polymarket has signaled an intent to bring resolution in-house, but as of April 30 it has not committed to a timeline. Its chief marketing officer confirmed plans for a POLY token in October 2025, headlined by CoinDesk as a possible move to “bring Oracle’s home.” On April 6, the company announced its largest infrastructure overhaul since launch, including a rebuilt trading engine, new smart contracts and a 1:1 USDC-backed collateral token called pUSD. CoinDesk framed it as Polymarket “taking control of its own trading and truth.” The exchange upgrade went live on April 28. None of the published documentation references a full UMA replacement.
Founder and CEO Shayne Coplan has been publicly relaxed about edge in his markets. “I think that people going and having an edge to the market is a good thing,” he said on CBS’s 60 Minutes in 2025. “Obviously, you need to curate them and you need to be really clear and stringent on where the line is drawn.” He has not yet commented publicly on the Clavicular dispute.
The new wave of complaints is coming from inside Polymarket’s own user base. Domahhhh, @TheGreekTrader and the rest of the cohort calling for change are themselves Polymarket bettors with positions in disputed markets, and that’s the disclosure that matters. They are also the people most directly exposed to the failure mode they are warning about. As of April 30, with $2.54 million sitting on the Yes side of a $16 million market the rules say should resolve No, the next UMA vote will decide whether they have a point.
